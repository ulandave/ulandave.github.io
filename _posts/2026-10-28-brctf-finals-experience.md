# BRCTF Finals: Beyond the Flags — My Cybersecurity Journey

There is a difference between learning cybersecurity and putting that knowledge to the test.

For a long time, I had been learning about web vulnerabilities, network reconnaissance, Linux privilege escalation, Active Directory, and the techniques security professionals use to identify and exploit weaknesses in systems.

Through platforms like Hack The Box and PortSwigger Web Security Academy, I had been building my skills one challenge at a time.

But BRCTF brought a different kind of experience.

Qualifying for the finals with my team was an important milestone in my cybersecurity journey. It gave me an opportunity to challenge myself, apply what I had learned, collaborate with others, and discover how much I still had to learn.

And that was exactly what made the experience valuable.

## Preparing for BRCTF

Preparing for BRCTF was more than memorizing commands or searching for exploits. I wanted to understand the methodology behind solving challenges.

I spent time improving my reconnaissance workflow, learning how to identify technologies running on target systems, investigating potential vulnerabilities, and understanding how an initial foothold could lead to further access in authorized lab environments.

I focused particularly on two areas:

- **Web application security:** Understanding how applications work, identifying attack surfaces, and investigating vulnerabilities that could compromise their security.
- **Active Directory security:** Learning how domain environments operate, how misconfigurations introduce security risks, and how to approach enterprise network assessments systematically.

One important lesson became clear: having a collection of tools is not enough.

What matters is understanding when to use them, interpreting their output, and knowing what to investigate next.

The goal was not simply to run commands. It was to develop a repeatable approach to solving problems.

## The BRCTF Experience

Participating in the BRCTF finals was an opportunity to put my preparation to the test.

CTF competitions push you beyond familiar tutorials. You encounter unfamiliar technologies, unexpected behaviours, and situations where your first approach might not work.

Sometimes, you have to return to the basics. Other times, you need to question an assumption, investigate a different service, or reconsider what the available evidence is telling you.

These experiences reinforce an important lesson:

**Cybersecurity is not just about knowing techniques. It is about thinking critically when the answer is not obvious.**

Working as part of a team also highlights the importance of communication. Sharing observations, explaining your reasoning, and approaching a problem from different perspectives can make a real difference.

Every challenge provides an opportunity to learn something new, whether you solve it immediately or need more time to understand it.

For me, BRCTF was not just about finding flags. It was about testing my thinking, strengthening my methodology, and learning to approach unfamiliar problems with patience.

## Lessons I Learned

Looking back at the experience, several lessons stand out.

### 1. Reconnaissance Is the Foundation

A rushed assessment can overlook important information.

Understanding the target environment, identifying exposed services, fingerprinting technologies, and mapping the attack surface establish a solid foundation for further investigation.

Good reconnaissance is not about collecting as much information as possible. It is about collecting the right information and using it to guide your next steps.

### 2. Methodology Matters More Than Tools

Tools can automate tasks and speed up investigations, but they cannot replace understanding.

Knowing why a scan is being performed, what its results mean, and which findings deserve further investigation is far more valuable than memorizing commands without understanding them.

I want to continue developing a structured methodology that I can apply across different environments.

### 3. Failure Is Part of the Learning Process

Not every technique works as expected. Not every vulnerability is exploitable, and not every promising lead leads somewhere useful.

Instead of treating these moments as wasted effort, I see them as opportunities to improve my reasoning.

Each unsuccessful attempt can help eliminate assumptions, improve my understanding of a system, and guide my next decision.

Sometimes, the most valuable lesson comes from understanding why something did not work.

### 4. Documentation Is a Technical Skill

One area I want to improve is documenting my work.

A good security assessment is not complete simply because a vulnerability has been discovered or access has been obtained. The process, evidence, impact, and remediation recommendations also matter.

Documenting challenges helps me revisit my reasoning, identify gaps in my understanding, and create useful references for future assessments.

It also allows me to share knowledge with other learners.

### 5. Teamwork Makes a Difference

Cybersecurity involves more than individual technical ability.

Communication, collaboration, time management, and the ability to explain technical findings are essential skills.

Working with teammates reminded me of the value of sharing ideas and learning from different approaches to the same problem.

Everyone brings a different perspective, and sometimes a fresh perspective is exactly what a difficult challenge needs.

## What Comes Next?

Completing BRCTF does not mean the learning stops. In many ways, it is another starting point.

As a Computer Science student at the University of Cape Coast, I want to continue building a strong technical foundation while developing practical cybersecurity skills.

My next steps include:

- Deepening my knowledge of web application vulnerabilities.
- Improving my Active Directory enumeration and security assessment skills.
- Practising Linux and Windows privilege escalation in authorized labs.
- Developing better technical documentation and professional reporting.
- Building a portfolio of practical projects, lab notes, and CTF writeups.
- Continuing to explore defensive security, penetration testing, and vulnerability research.

I also want to become better at explaining not only *how* a vulnerability works, but *why* it exists, what makes it exploitable, and how it can be prevented.

That understanding is essential for becoming a better security practitioner.

## Final Thoughts

BRCTF was another step in a journey that I know will take time, consistency, and a willingness to keep learning.

I am still growing, still making mistakes, and still discovering new areas of cybersecurity that challenge my understanding.

But that is what makes this field exciting.

There is always something new to investigate, another concept to understand, and another opportunity to improve.

For me, the real achievement is not just participating in a competition. It is coming away with a better understanding of my strengths, the areas I need to improve, and the work required to become the cybersecurity professional I aspire to be.

**The flags may be behind me, but the learning continues.**

I will continue documenting my cybersecurity journey, sharing lessons from CTF challenges, exploring vulnerabilities in authorized environments, and building projects that reflect what I learn along the way.

Thank you for being part of the journey.

*Keep learning. Keep testing.**
